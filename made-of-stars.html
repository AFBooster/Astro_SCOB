Chapter 2

🍳 Six cosmic kitchens

The old one-line story — “stars make the elements and supernovae scatter them” — is only part of the truth. Astronomers now count six different kitchens, and some are surprising: most of the gold on Earth was never inside a supernova at all.

💥

The Big Bang

The first 20 minutes · 13.8 billion years ago

For a few minutes the whole universe was a nuclear furnace. Protons and neutrons fused into helium — then the universe expanded, cooled, and the cooking stopped. Out came about three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium by weight, plus a trace of lithium. Nothing heavier.

H hydrogenHe heliumLi a little lithium

Giant stars that explode

Stars over ~8 times the Sun’s mass · core-collapse supernovae

A giant star fuses ever-heavier elements in its core — helium, then carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon — until it is left with iron, which gives out no energy when fused. The core collapses in under a second and the star blows itself apart, flinging its new elements into space and cooking more in the blast.

O oxygenNe neonMg magnesiumSi siliconS sulfurCa calciumP phosphorusNa sodiumK potassium+ part of the C & Fe

Supernova 1987A made about 0.07 Suns’ worth of radioactive nickel that decayed into iron — some 23,000 Earths of it.

💨

Sun-like stars that die gently

Stars of about 1–8 Suns · red giants → planetary nebulae

Smaller stars never explode. As bloated red giants they slow-cook heavy elements by catching neutrons one at a time, then puff their outer layers — rich in carbon, nitrogen and those heavy atoms — gently into space as a glowing planetary nebula, leaving a white-dwarf ember behind. This quiet kitchen made about half of everything heavier than iron.

C carbonN nitrogenSr strontiumBa bariumPb lead+ half the heavy elements

The proof came in 1952, when technetium — an element that decays away within a few million years — was found in the light of red giants. It can only be there if the star is making it. (How do we read what is in a star’s light?)

Exploding white dwarfs

Type Ia supernovae · dead stars in close pairs

A white dwarf is the Earth-sized ember of a dead Sun-like star. Give it a close companion and it can steal gas (or merge with another white dwarf) until it nears about 1.4 times the Sun’s mass — then it detonates completely, leaving nothing behind but a cloud of new elements, above all iron.

Fe about half the ironMn manganeseNi nickelCr chromium+ some Si, S, Ca

Tycho Brahe’s “new star” of 1572 was one of these — confirmed in 2008 from its light echo, still bouncing off distant dust clouds four centuries later.

💫

Colliding neutron stars

Kilonovae · the gold mines of the universe

A neutron star is the city-sized core left behind when a giant star explodes. When two of them orbit each other they slowly spiral together and smash. The wreckage is so flooded with neutrons that atoms swell all the way up to gold, platinum and uranium in about a second.

Au goldPt platinumU uraniumI iodineEu europium+ many rare earths

On 17 August 2017 detectors felt the gravitational waves of one such crash, 130 million light-years away (GW170817), and telescopes then watched the glow of freshly made heavy elements. One estimate: about 16,000 Earths’ worth of heavy elements, around 10 Earths of them gold and platinum. Rare, fast-spinning collapsing stars may add some gold too — the split is still being worked out.

Cosmic-ray collisions

Atoms chipped apart in space

Cosmic rays are atomic nuclei flung out by supernovae at nearly the speed of light. When one slams into a carbon or oxygen atom drifting in space, it chips off small fragments. That is where nearly all beryllium and boron, and some lithium, come from — stars cannot make them; they destroy them.

Be berylliumB boronLi some lithium
You can’t point a telescope at this kitchen — but it reaches you: cosmic rays striking our atmosphere make particles called muons, and a steady stream of them passes through your body every second.
🧪

…and a tiny seventh kitchen: us

Nuclear reactors & particle accelerators · since 1937

Technetium and promethium were made in stars too, but every form of them decays away, so all the star-made atoms are long gone. Today we make them — and every element beyond uranium — in reactors and accelerators. The speck of americium inside most smoke alarms is one.

Why it all stops at iron

Think of a valley. Fusing light nuclei together rolls them downhill: every step releases energy, and that energy is what makes a star shine. Iron, with its neighbour nickel, sits at the very bottom. Fusing iron into anything heavier means pushing uphill — it costs energy instead of making it. So the moment a giant star’s core turns to iron, its power is cut, and gravity wins.

The far side of the valley is how nuclear power works: splitting (fission) uranium also rolls downhill towards iron, and releases energy. Vertical scale squeezed so the whole curve fits.

Chapter 4

⚛️ Beyond iron: the slow lane and the fast lane

If fusion stops at iron, how do gold and uranium exist? Not by fusing — by catching neutrons. A nucleus swallows a neutron; sooner or later a neutron inside it turns into a proton, and the atom becomes the next element up. There are two ways to climb.

Both start at iron (26 protons).
🐢 Slow lane — the “s-process”

Inside dying red giants. Neutrons trickle in, roughly one every few decades or centuries, so each new nucleus has time to settle before the next arrives. The climb takes thousands of years — and gets stuck at lead and bismuth.

Sr strontiumBa bariumPb leadBi bismuth
🐇 Fast lane — the “r-process”

In colliding neutron stars. A flood of neutrons: hundreds caught in under a second, building wildly overweight nuclei that then decay into gold, platinum, iodine, europium, thorium and uranium.

I iodineEu europiumPt platinumAu goldU uranium
💡 Every atom of uranium and thorium on Earth came from the fast lane — the slow lane can’t get past bismuth. Their slow radioactive decay still helps keep the inside of our planet hot.
Chapter 5

♻️ The great recycling: the cycle that made the Sun

No single star makes much on its own. The Milky Way builds heavy elements the way a garden builds soil: stars are born from gas, cook it, and hand it back a little richer — over and over. Run the cycle.

💡 The Sun’s ancestors were mostly gentle. A 2024 study counted the stars whose ashes ended up in our Solar System: about 93 in every 100 were ordinary stars that died quietly, about 6 were giants that exploded, and fewer than 1 was an exploding white dwarf.

There were no neat “generations” in reality — stars of every size were being born and dying at once for nine billion years before the Sun. The laps here are schematic.

Chapter 7

🧍 You are made of star stuff

An average 70 kg adult, one square for every 1%. Count by weight or by number of atoms, and colour it by element or by where each atom was made.

    💡 It is the same for all life. Every living thing is built mostly from six elements — carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur. One came from the Big Bang; the other five from stars. The magnesium at the heart of every chlorophyll molecule in a leaf was made in a giant star; the iodine your thyroid needs, in colliding neutron stars.

    “We are made of star-stuff.” — Carl Sagan, Cosmos (1980)

    Amounts are averaged reference values for a 70 kg adult; real bodies vary. By weight hydrogen is only a tenth of you — but it is the lightest atom, so it is six in every ten of your atoms.

    Chapter 8

    🌍 The Earth, and the things in your hand

    The whole Earth, by weight

    When the young Earth was molten, its iron sank to the centre to form the core — and dragged nearly all the gold and platinum down with it. There are enough precious metals in the core to coat the whole planet four metres deep. The gold we mine arrived later, on meteorites that kept raining down for hundreds of millions of years after the core had formed.

    💡 All the gold ever mined — about 222,600 tonnes — would fit in a cube 22.6 m on each side. The neutron-star crash of 2017 made around 10 Earths’ worth of gold and platinum in one go.

    Chapter 10

    🤔 Myths, and what we still don’t know

    🚫 “All the gold came from supernovae.” Probably not most of it. Ordinary supernovae turn out to be poor at making gold; the evidence points to colliding neutron stars (caught in the act in 2017), perhaps helped by rare, extreme collapsing stars.
    🚫 “Stars fuse their way up to gold.” Fusion stops at iron. Everything heavier is built by catching neutrons — slowly in red giants, or in a flash when neutron stars collide.
    🚫 “The Sun will explode one day.” It is far too small. In about five billion years it will swell into a red giant, puff its outer layers gently into space — handing some carbon and nitrogen back to the galaxy — and settle down as a white dwarf.
    🚫 “We are 100% stardust.” Almost: about 90% of your weight was made in stars. But the hydrogen in you — six in every ten of your atoms — is older than any star. It came straight from the Big Bang.
    🔬 Still being worked out: how much of the gold comes from neutron-star collisions and how much from rare, exotic supernovae; what the very first stars were like (none has ever been seen directly); and the “lithium problem” — the Big Bang should have made about three times more lithium than the oldest stars show.
