🌈 Reading Starlight

How scientists know what a star is made of, how hot it is and how fast it is moving — without ever going there. Science Centre Observatory, Jurong.

The short version

Chapter 1

🔦 The light lab: three kinds of rainbow

In 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen worked out the rules. Pick a light source below and watch what reaches the rainbow. Each dot is a little packet of light — a photon — with its own colour.

1 · Continuous spectrumA hot, dense glowing thing — a light-bulb filament, molten metal, the deep layers of a star — gives every colour: a smooth rainbow.
2 · Absorption linesShine that light through cooler gas and the gas takes out its own colours, leaving dark lines. Starlight looks like this: the star’s outer layers are the gas.
3 · Emission linesHot, thin gas on its own glows only in those same colours: bright lines on black. Neon signs and glowing nebulae look like this.

The lab is drawn for clarity, not to scale. Notice in the middle mode that the gas does not destroy the light it catches — it sends it back out in random directions, which is why the same gas, seen from the side, glows with bright lines.

Chapter 2

🪜 Why only certain colours? The atom’s ladder

An atom’s electron can only sit on certain “rungs” of energy, like a ladder with no in-between steps. When it drops from a higher rung to a lower one, it throws out a photon carrying exactly the energy of that drop — and energy means colour. Hydrogen’s rungs give it four visible colours.

💡 The colours are not guesses — they come out of one formula. In 1885 a Swiss schoolteacher, Johann Balmer, found the pattern in hydrogen’s four visible lines; the page works out every wavelength above from the modern version of his rule (the Rydberg formula). Drops to the bottom rung give ultraviolet light, and drops to the third rung infrared, both invisible to our eyes but seen by telescopes.

👩‍🔬
The woman who found what stars are made of. Around 1900 Annie Jump Cannon and the women “computers” of Harvard sorted hundreds of thousands of star spectra by eye into these classes — Cannon alone classified some 350,000. In 1925 Cecilia Payne used the new physics of atoms to show why the spectra differ: mostly temperature, not ingredients. Her surprise was that stars are made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. Senior astronomers doubted it, and she was persuaded to call her result “almost certainly not real” — but within four years it was confirmed. Today it is the bedrock of astronomy.
Chapter 6

🚀 Moving lines: the Doppler shift

When a star moves towards us its light waves are squashed together, so every line slides a little towards blue. When it moves away they are stretched towards red. It is the same effect that makes an ambulance siren drop in pitch as it passes. Measure the slide and you have the speed.

🧾 One spectrum, six answers. What it is made of — which barcodes appear. How hot — its colour, and which lines are strong. How fast it moves towards or away from us — the shift. How fast it spins — spinning smears each line wider. Its magnetism — magnetic fields split lines in two (how George Ellery Hale found in 1908 that sunspots are giant magnets). Hidden company — lines that wobble back and forth betray an unseen star or planet tugging on it.

🔭 Spectroscopy at SCOB tonight

The Observatory uses this idea every time its Seestar S30 photographs a nebula from the middle of Jurong. Its built-in dual-band filter lets through just two narrow slices of colour — a 30 nm window around the green-blue oxygen line (OIII, 500.7 nm) and a 20 nm window around the red hydrogen line (Hα, 656.3 nm) — and blocks the rest.

Chapter 8

🛠️ Make your own spectroscope

The tiny tracks on a CD or DVD split light just like a prism. With a box and some tape you can see the barcodes of the lamps around you — tonight.

  1. Find a small box (a tea or tissue box works) and an old CD or DVD you no longer need. A DVD’s tracks are closer together, so it spreads the colours wider.
  2. At one end cut a window about 2 cm tall and cover it with two pieces of card or aluminium foil, taped so that only a very thin slit is left between them — about 1 mm. The thinner the slit, the sharper the lines.
  3. Tape the disc inside the opposite end, shiny side facing the slit, tilted at about 45°. Cut a peephole in the top or side of the box above the disc.
  4. Point the slit at a lamp and look through the peephole at the disc. Tilt the box until a rainbow appears. Try every lamp you can find.
  5. Compare what you see with the barcodes below. A torch or a filament bulb gives a smooth rainbow; fluorescent tubes and neon signs give bright lines.
⚠️ Never point your spectroscope — or anything else — at the Sun. It can permanently damage your eyes. For sunlight, point the slit at a bright white cloud or a sunlit sheet of paper. With a really fine slit you may even glimpse Fraunhofer’s dark lines.

What the lamps around you look like

On the deck, astronomers do the same with a tiny diffraction grating such as the Star Analyser, which screws into a telescope’s eyepiece or camera like a filter and turns every star into a short spectrum.

Chapter 9

🏛️ How we learned to read starlight

1666
Isaac Newton splits sunlight with a prism and calls the band of colours a “spectrum”.
1802
William Wollaston notices a few dark gaps in the Sun’s spectrum — and thinks they are just the borders between colours.
1814
Joseph von Fraunhofer, a glass-maker, maps 574 dark lines and labels the strongest A to K. He also sees that stars such as Sirius have different patterns from the Sun.
1859
Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen show that each element has its own lines, and that the Sun’s dark lines mean sodium, iron and more are in the Sun. Within two years they discover two new elements by their lines alone: caesium (“sky-blue”) and rubidium (“deep red”).
1864
William Huggins turns a spectroscope on the Cat’s Eye Nebula and sees bright lines, not a rainbow: it is glowing gas, not a swarm of stars.
1868
During an eclipse Pierre Janssen, and soon after Norman Lockyer, see a yellow line in the Sun that matches no known element. Lockyer names it helium, after the Greek for Sun. William Ramsay finally finds helium on Earth in 1895.
1901
Annie Jump Cannon puts the star classes into the order O B A F G K M, still used today.
1908
George Ellery Hale sees sunspot lines split in two: sunspots are giant magnets.
1912
Vesto Slipher measures the first speed of a galaxy: Andromeda is coming towards us at about 300 km/s. Most of the others he measures are rushing away.
1925
Cecilia Payne shows that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
1927
Ira Bowen solves the mystery of “nebulium”, a supposed new element glowing green in nebulae: it is ordinary oxygen, giving off light that is only possible in near-empty space.
1929
Edwin Hubble puts Slipher’s speeds together with distances: the farther a galaxy, the faster it moves away. The universe is expanding.
1963
Maarten Schmidt realises that the strange lines of 3C 273 are just hydrogen, stretched by 16%: it is a quasar, far beyond our galaxy.
1995
Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz see 51 Pegasi’s lines wobble by about 56 m/s every four days: the first planet found around a Sun-like star (Nobel Prize 2019).
2022
The James Webb Space Telescope finds carbon dioxide in the air of WASP-39 b, a planet 700 light-years away, by reading the starlight that passes through its atmosphere.
