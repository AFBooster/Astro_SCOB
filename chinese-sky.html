Long before the Greek constellations reached China, the sky here was mapped as an imperial court — an emperor, his family and ministers, each a small group of stars called a star official (星官).

Where Western astronomy is built on the ecliptic and its zodiac, the Chinese sky is equatorial and pole-centred: the unmoving north pole is the throne, and the whole sky wheels around it.

Why twenty-eight? The Moon circles the stars in about 27.3 days, resting each night against a new patch of sky — a 宿 xiù, a "lodge". Rounded off, that gives 28 stations, one per night.

Why four animals? The 28 lodges group into four sevens, one per direction and season. Joined up, each quarter forms a beast: the Azure Dragon of spring in the east, the Vermilion Bird of summer in the south, the White Tiger of autumn in the west, the Black Tortoise of winter in the north — so the season could be read from whichever ruled the dusk.
