In Indian astronomy — Jyotiṣa, "the science of light" — the sky is measured not by the Sun but by the Moon. Its monthly path is divided into 27 equal segments called नक्षत्र nakshatras, or lunar mansions: the 27 "homes" the Moon rests in, one each night.

Each nakshatra is a small patch of sky marked by a bright junction star (योगतारा yogatārā), and carries its own deity, symbol, temperament and ruling planet. Together they form a calendar, a compass and a system of meaning written across the stars.

Why twenty-seven? The Moon circles the stars in about 27.3 days. Rounded down, that gives 27 stations — so each night the Moon "lodges" in the next nakshatra, shifting roughly 13° eastward. (A 28th, Abhijit, near the star Vega, is sometimes added for ritual timing.)

Why nine lords? The 27 nakshatras cycle three times through nine planetary rulers (the नवग्रह Navagraha) — Ketu, Venus, Sun, Moon, Mars, Rahu, Jupiter, Saturn, Mercury. This sequence sets the great Vimśottarī cycle of life-periods, the backbone of Indian astrology.

🐉 A shared idea. The Chinese sky divides the same band into 28 mansions (宿 xiù) and the Indian sky into 27 — two great civilisations independently mapped the Moon's path into "lodges". Many nakshatras and mansions even share the same stars. Compare them in The Chinese Sky →

The 12 Rāśi — signs of the zodiac राशि

The same circle of sky is also split into 12 signs of 30° each. Since 27 into 12 doesn't divide evenly, each sign holds 2¼ nakshatras (9 quarter-steps called पद padas). Tap a sign to see which nakshatras fall inside it.

☀️ Sidereal vs tropical. Indian astrology uses the sidereal zodiac — fixed to the actual stars. Western (horoscope) astrology uses the tropical zodiac — fixed to the seasons. Slow wobbling of Earth's axis (precession) has pulled these apart by about 24° today, the अयनांश ayanāṁśa. That's why your "Vedic sign" is usually one sign earlier than your familiar one.

The plotted asterisms show each nakshatra's junction star (yogatārā) and, where the mansion is a well-known figure, its brighter members to about magnitude 5 (Jurong's practical city-sky limit), at Hipparcos J2000 positions. Junction-star identifications follow the standard yogatārā list (after the Sūrya Siddhānta and B. V. Raman), cross-checked to modern catalogues; deities, symbols, ganas and planetary lords follow classical Jyotiṣa (Parāśara tradition). Twenty-one of the 27 junction stars are shared with the Chinese mansions and use the same coordinates as The Chinese Sky. Sidereal longitudes use the Lahiri (Chitrapakṣa) ayanāṁśa.
