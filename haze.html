Why haze hurts astronomy twice over

1 · It dims the stars

Smoke particles absorb and scatter starlight out of the beam before it reaches the eyepiece. Crucially this scales with how much air you look through — an object 20° above the horizon is seen through roughly three times the atmosphere of one overhead, so it loses three times as much. This is why haze finishes off a low Sagittarius globular long before it touches something at the zenith, and why "wait for it to rise higher" is even better advice on a hazy night than a clear one.

2 · It brightens the sky

Every particle that scatters starlight away also scatters Jurong's streetlights back down at you. Under a Bortle 8–9 sky there is a great deal of light to scatter, so the background gets brighter at the same time as the stars get fainter — the floor rises while the ceiling drops. On a bad night this is the larger of the two effects, and it is why a hazy Singapore sky looks orange rather than merely dim.

The practical rule for the deck: haze is a contrast problem, not a brightness problem. Anything that beats the sky by a wide margin — the Moon, Jupiter, Saturn, Venus, bright double stars, tight clusters — sails through almost untouched, though it will soften at high power. Anything faint and spread out — nebulae, galaxies, the Milky Way — loses first and loses most. On a hazy Friday, run the session on the Solar System and the doubles and save the faint fuzzies for a clear week.

Does the Seestar's dual-band filter help? Partly. The Hα/OIII filter rejects most of the added sky glow, so an emission nebula still stacks usefully in haze that would defeat the eye. But no filter can recover light that was scattered out of the beam on the way in — the target itself is genuinely fainter, so exposures need to be longer for the same result. Broadband targets (galaxies, reflection nebulae) get no help at all.

Does haze affect seeing? Not directly. Seeing is about turbulence — the steadiness of the air — and a smoke layer is not necessarily turbulent. In practice hazy Singapore evenings are often calm, so planetary detail can hold up well even as the deep sky disappears. That is another reason a hazy night is a Solar System night.

The bands, and what they mean for a public session

A Friday night at SCOB puts visitors of every age on an open deck for a couple of hours. That is a health question as much as an observing one, and it is keyed to the 24-hour PSI, because that is the index NEA's official advisories use — the 1-hour PM2.5 bands are published for information only and carry no advisory of their own.

Health advice is the National Environment Agency's; the session guidance is SCOB's own reading of it and is not a substitute for a duty officer's judgement or any Science Centre policy. Check haze.gov.sg for the official advisory and forecast on the night.
