The constellations most of the world knows today are the Greek ones — 48 figures catalogued by Ptolemy in his Almagest around 150 CE, grown over the centuries to the modern 88. But the Greeks did not invent them from nothing: they inherited many from Babylon and wrapped them in their own glittering mythology.

So the Greek sky is best read as a set of stories. A single tragedy plays out across six constellations — vain Queen Cassiopeia, her daughter Andromeda chained to the rock, the sea-monster Cetus, and the hero Perseus on winged Pegasus who saves her.

The zodiac carries the Sun through the Bull, the Lion, the Scorpion; Orion the hunter strides the winter sky with his dogs; and among the gods' own things hang the Great Bear, the Lyre of Orpheus and the Swan.
