🎚️ Imaging & Visual Filters

What every filter does, the light it isolates and when to reach for it — for imaging and the eyepiece. Science Centre Observatory, Jurong.

Every astro filter does one of two jobs: let the good light in (the glow of a nebula, the reflected light off a planet) or keep the bad light out (Singapore's heavy streetlight glow, scattered moonlight, infrared your optics can't focus). The right filter can lift a target out of an orange city sky that swallows it in a plain photograph.

🌈 The four emission lines that matter

Different objects glow at different wavelengths. Narrowband filters isolate slivers just a few nanometres (nm) wide around these.

Hydrogen-alpha (Hα) and sulphur-II (SII) sit in the deep red; doubly-ionised oxygen (OIII) and hydrogen-beta (Hβ) sit in the blue-green.

📷 The fork in the road: mono vs one-shot-colour

Which filters you buy depends entirely on your camera.

Monochrome camera

Records pure brightness. You shoot the target repeatedly through separate filters — R, G, B, and/or Hα, OIII, SII — then combine in software. Highest resolution and full control, but needs a filter wheel and more time.

One-shot-colour (OSC / DSLR / smart scope)

Has a built-in colour matrix, so it captures colour in a single exposure. You screw one filter in front and go — usually a light-pollution or dual-band filter. Simpler, faster, no wheel. The Observatory's Seestar works this way.

Rule of thumb: mono imagers buy individual line filters (Hα, OIII, SII) and LRGB sets; OSC imagers buy combination filters (dual-band, tri-band, broadband) that pass several useful bands at once.

Wide passbands that shape or clean up the whole visible spectrum rather than isolating one line.

⚠️ Broadband light-pollution filters can't work miracles. Modern LED streetlights emit a broad, continuous spectrum, so these help far less than in the old sodium-lamp era. From a sky as bright as Singapore's, narrowband is the real answer.

🎯 Narrowband line filters

Very narrow passbands (3–12 nm) centred on one emission line — the heart of nebula imaging.

Because they reject almost the entire spectrum except one thin slice, narrowband filters cut light pollution and even moonlight to near-irrelevance — you can image bright emission nebulae from the city under a full Moon. They only work on emission targets (nebulae glowing at specific lines), not on galaxies or star clusters.

Hα · 656.3 nm

Hydrogen-alpha

Deep red. The most abundant, highest-signal line. The single most useful narrowband filter and a great first buy.

OIII · 500.7 nm

Oxygen-III

Teal blue-green. Planetary nebulae, supernova remnants, delicate outer shells. Usually the second filter added.

SII · 671.6 nm

Sulphur-II

Deep red, just beyond Hα. Faint and slow, but the third channel of the classic Hubble palette.

Hβ · 486.1 nmNII · 658.4 nm Hydrogen-beta helps with a few faint objects; NII sits so close to Hα that only very narrow filters separate them.

Narrower = darker sky, but slower & pricier. A 3 nm filter gives blacker backgrounds and better Moon rejection than a 7 nm, but passes less light (longer exposures) and costs more — and needs a well-corrected, not-too-fast optical system.

🔀 Dual- & multi-band filters (for OSC)

The clever compromise that brings narrowband to one-shot-colour cameras.

These pass two or more narrow bands at once — most commonly Hα and OIII. On a colour sensor the red pixels catch the Hα and the blue-green pixels catch the OIII, so a single exposure gives a colourful nebula with heavy light-pollution rejection. No filter wheel, no separate subs.

TypeBandsNotes
Dual-bandHα + OIIIThe go-to OSC nebula filter; sold at ~7 nm and ~3 nm widths.
Tri-bandHα + Hβ + OIIIAdds the Hβ region for a little more blue signal.
Quad-bandHα + SII + Hβ + OIIIAll four workhorse lines for fuller nebula colour on OSC.
Naming can mislead. "L-eNhance", "L-eXtreme", "Duo-Band", "ALP-T" and the like are brand names — always check the actual bands and bandwidth in the spec sheet, not the marketing label.

🎨 Narrowband palettes

Narrowband data is monochrome per line, so you choose which line becomes which colour channel. It's a creative choice, not a physical one.

🟥🟩🟦 LRGB filters (mono broadband colour)

The classic way a mono camera makes a true-colour image.

Shoot four stacks and combine: a high-detail Luminance layer for sharpness and depth, plus Red, Green and Blue for colour. This is the standard approach for galaxies, clusters and reflection nebulae — broadband targets where narrowband doesn't apply. Many advanced images blend both: LRGB for natural star colour, with Hα mixed into red to boost nebulosity.

L — brightness/detail master (most exposure time)   R   G   B — the colour channels.

🪐 Planetary & lunar filters

A different world — fast video of small, bright targets, fighting atmospheric blur rather than light pollution.

☀️ Solar filters

For imaging the Sun — the one category where a mistake causes instant, permanent damage.

⚠️ Never point a telescope, finder or camera at the Sun without a certified full-aperture solar filter fitted over the front of the instrument. Ordinary ND, polarising or screw-in "sun" eyepiece filters are not safe and can shatter. Eye and sensor damage is instantaneous. See the Daytime / Solar mode page for the full safety checklist.
FilterWhat you see
White-light (front) — glass or Baader filmThe photosphere: sunspots, granulation. Attaches to the front aperture, blocks ~99.999% of light.

Calcium-K (393 nm)The chromosphere in violet — plage and active regions.
Herschel wedgeA prism (refractors only) for very high-contrast white-light imaging.

📏 Understanding bandwidth (FWHM)

The single number that separates a budget filter from a premium one.

Bandwidth — quoted as FWHM (full width at half maximum, in nm) — is how wide a slice of spectrum the filter passes. Narrower means darker skies and better rejection, but lower throughput and higher cost.

WidthCharacterBest when
12 nm +Bright, forgiving, fast, cheapDark skies, fast optics, beginners
6–7 nmThe popular sweet spotSuburban skies, most amateur setups
3 nm −Blackest sky, best Moon/LP rejectionHeavy light pollution, full Moon, premium rigs
Faster than f/4? Mind the band-shift. Very narrow filters shift their passband under steep light cones from fast optics, dimming the field edges. Filter makers publish which f-ratios each design tolerates — check before pairing a 3 nm filter with a very fast astrograph.

Match the filter size to your sensor. Filters come as 1.25"/2" round mounted, or unmounted 31/36/50 mm for wheels. Undersized filters vignette large sensors — size to the camera, not just the scope.

🔭 For SCOB sessions

Under Jurong's bright, humid, light-polluted sky the most useful filters are: an ND or polarising filter to tame lunar glare on public Moon nights; colour Wratten filters and an IR-pass for planetary contrast when the seeing is soft; and a certified front solar filter for daytime Sun sessions. For deep-sky imaging from the deck, a dual-band (Hα+OIII) filter is the only kind that meaningfully cuts through the city glow — which is exactly why the Observatory's Seestar smart telescope relies on a built-in dual-band filter to pull nebulae out of the orange sky.
