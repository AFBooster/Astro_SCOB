High in the Andes, the Inca read the sky in two ways at once. Like everyone, they joined bright stars into figures. But they also did something almost no other people did: they saw constellations in the dark — in the black dust-lanes that split the glowing band of the Milky Way.

That band was Mayu, the celestial river. Into its dark pools came animals to drink, their silhouettes blotting out the light: Yacana the great Llama, her eyes the brilliant stars α and β Centauri; Yutu the partridge; the Fox, the Toad and the winding Serpent.

The bright figures mattered too — above all Qullqa, the Storehouse (our Pleiades), whose June appearance was studied each year to forecast the rains and time the planting; and Chakana, the Southern Cross, the most sacred shape in the Andes.

🌑 A sky of shadows. Dark-cloud constellations are the Inca's great gift to world astronomy. Because they have no bright stars, most have no counterpart in the star-based Chinese or Indian systems — you can only see them where the Milky Way rides high and dark, as it does from Singapore.

Mayu & the harvest star — reading the river & the Pleiades

Mayu, the celestial river. The Inca saw the Milky Way as a great river that watered both sky and Earth. It runs at an angle across the heavens, and over a night and a year it wheels around, its dark animals rising and setting in a fixed order — a living calendar in shadow.

Two rivers, one water. The Inca believed the celestial Mayu and the earthly rivers were one system: the Sun, crossing the Milky Way, was thought to scoop up water and carry it round to fall again as rain.

The harvest star. Each June, farmers across the Andes still judge how bright and clear Qullqa (the Pleiades) looks at its dawn rising. A clear, bright cluster promises good rains and an early planting; a dim, hazy one warns of drought and a late one. Modern science has found the trick works — thin high cloud from El Niño years really does dim the Pleiades.

🦙 Yacana comes to drink. At midnight in the wet season the dark Llama was said to descend and drink from the sea, keeping the world from flooding — then climb back into Mayu before dawn. Watch her rise on the tonight page.
